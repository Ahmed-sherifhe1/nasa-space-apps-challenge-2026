2. The Eastern Harbor of Alexandria, Egypt — The Submerged Royal Quarters
The Historical Context & Narrative
Founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BCE, ancient Alexandria was the intellectual and architectural crown jewel of the Mediterranean world. Its Eastern Harbor was framed by the Pharos Lighthouse (one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World), the grand Royal Quarters of the Ptolemaic dynasty, the Timonium (Mark Antony’s private retreat), and the Caesareum temple complex. For centuries, travelers marveled at the white limestone palaces and colossal statues rising directly out of the coastal waters.

However, between the 4th and 14th centuries CE, this entire coastal landscape vanished beneath the waves. Today, divers exploring the murky waters of the Eastern Harbor swim over an intact, submerged cityscape resting 5 to 8 meters below sea level—complete with toppled granite obelisks, sphinxes, paved streets, and temple columns preserved in the seabed silt.

Geotechnical & Hydrological Causes
Seismic-Induced Soil Liquefaction: Alexandria was not built on solid bedrock; it sits on the western edge of the Nile Delta, composed of soft, water-saturated alluvial clay, silt, and sand. During major Eastern Mediterranean earthquakes—most notably the catastrophic 365 CE Crete earthquake and the 1303 CE earthquake—intense seismic shaking caused the water-logged sediment beneath the city to lose its shear strength and behave like a liquid. Monumental stone palaces and heavy statues literally sank into the mud under their own weight.

Tsunami Scouring: The 365 CE tremor generated a massive tsunami that first drained the harbor dry before smashing back into the coastline with devastating force. The receding waters stripped away supporting coastal sediments, undermining the foundations of harbor walls and waterfront palaces.

Long-Term Tectonic Subsidence & Sea-Level Rise: The Nile Delta is naturally sinking at a rate of several millimeters per year due to the sheer weight of accumulated river sediment and tectonic plate movement along the Hellenic Arc. Combined with post-glacial eustatic sea-level rise over the last two millennia, the ancient shoreline dropped permanently beneath the Mediterranean.
